UK Clinical Psychology Training

The clinical psychologists of tomorrow look different from those of a decade ago

Applications are up 57 per cent. The profession is broadening on race, sexuality and disability — and is now more ethnically diverse than the UK working-age population, and dramatically more LGB+. The ethnic success-rate gap that once made a White applicant three times as likely to be accepted as a Black one has been near-closed since 2021. But the largest source of unequal outcomes in the data is one the profession barely discusses: age.

Clinical psychology is one of the most competitive postgraduate paths in the UK. Each year, thousands of applicants chase a few hundred funded training places on doctoral courses run by the NHS. Get in, and you are on a salaried three-year training programme. Miss out, and many spend years working as assistant psychologists or research assistants, waiting for another shot.

For ten successive years the Clearing House, which coordinates applications nationally, has published anonymised demographic data on every applicant and every successful candidate. Taken together, those ten slices tell a story of a profession in motion — and a profession with some immovable features.

Three things stand out. First, demand has surged: applications have grown more than half again, and most of that growth is recent. Second, the profile of who is applying has shifted meaningfully — fewer White, fewer straight, more openly disabled candidates than at any point in the recent past. Third, the most consequential change of all may be the one that just stopped: between 2016 and 2022, the gap in success rates between White and minority applicants closed almost completely. In 2024 it began to widen again.

And a fourth, from setting the data against the 2021 UK Census: the historic image of clinical psychology as a famously white profession does not survive contact with the 2024 applicant pool. Ethnic minority candidates now exceed their share of the working-age population at the application stage. The diversity gap, where it remains, sits downstream — in differential success rates, in who gets accepted, and in the senior ranks of the profession that this dataset does not see.

01 / DemandA surge that began before the pandemic — and accelerated after it

Clinical psychology training has always been hard to get into. But the bottleneck has tightened. In 2015, just over 3,500 people applied for the year's intake. In 2024, that number was almost 5,600 — a 57 per cent rise in nine years.

Acceptances have grown too. Health Education England and its successor body expanded commissioned places sharply during and after the pandemic, particularly in 2020 and 2021. Training places nearly doubled at their peak. But the application pool grew faster than the system could absorb, and in 2024 acceptances dipped while applications continued to climb.

The result is a competition ratio that swung sharply, then swung back. In 2019, just over 15 per cent of applicants were offered a place. By 2022, that had risen to a high of 25 per cent — the friendliest entry conditions in the dataset. In 2024 it dropped back to 20.6 per cent.

For applicants, the recent picture is not so much "easier to get in" as "more competition for the same enlarged pie that is now starting to shrink again".

The story above is a story about supply, not just demand. The clinical psychology workforce was an explicit priority in NHS England's expansion of psychological professions, with cohort sizes growing under the long-term workforce plan. The 2022 peak — when one in four applicants received a place — coincided with that high-water mark.

Whether 2024's reversal is the start of a sustained re-tightening or a single year's adjustment will only become clear with future cohorts. But the underlying signal in applications is unambiguous: more people want to become clinical psychologists than ever.

02 / EthnicityA gap that was enormous, and is now hard to see

In 2015, a White applicant to clinical psychology training was more than three times as likely to get a place as a Black applicant. By 2021, the two groups were succeeding at the same rate. From 2021 onwards the gap between White and pooled non-White applicants has been statistically indistinguishable from zero in every year except 2023, and even there only barely.

The figures from the pre-2020 era are stark. In 2015 and 2016, White applicants received offers at 17 per cent. Black applicants received offers at 5 to 6 per cent; Asian applicants at 12 to 14 per cent. In 2017, 2018 and 2019, Asian success rates fell as low as 7 to 9 per cent. The system was, by these numbers alone, working very differently for different groups of candidates.

Then it changed. By 2021, every major ethnic group was succeeding at roughly comparable rates — and Black applicants were, for the first time in the dataset, succeeding at exactly the same rate as White applicants: both at 22 per cent. In 2022, every major ethnic group's success rate sat within a four-point band of 22 to 26 per cent. It was a remarkable shift, and it tracked the most aggressive expansion of training places in recent memory.

One pattern that the standard "minority disadvantage" framing has always missed is worth naming here. Mixed-heritage applicants have succeeded at White-or-higher rates throughout the dataset — peaking at 30.5 per cent in 2023, several points above the White rate. The historic gap was specifically about Asian and Black applicants underperforming a White-and-Mixed reference line, not about minorities as a category. Aggregating "non-White" into a single comparison group, as the chart below does, obscures this — but the prose context is important: the convergence story is really about two groups catching up to a reference rate that two other groups (White and Mixed) had set throughout.

The 2023 widening — 4.8 points between White and Black applicants — looked at the time like a reversal. The chart below shows what happens when you pool all non-White applicants into one line, attach a confidence interval to each year, and ask whether the apparent gap could plausibly be noise. The answer for 2023 is "just barely not noise". The answer for 2021, 2022 and 2024 is "yes, plausibly noise." Treating the 2022–2024 sequence as a "reopening followed by partial closure" turns out to be reading signal into noise.

One methodological note before the chart. The Clearing House data is closer to a census of consenting applicants than a random sample, so the confidence intervals here should be read as a noise envelope — the range of rates one would plausibly observe if the admissions process were rerun under similar conditions — rather than as classical sampling intervals.

Across the full ten years, roughly one in five White applicants received a place against roughly one in six non-White applicants — a cumulative differential of three percentage points across 42,000 applications. That cumulative figure conceals year-by-year variation that is much more uncertain than the headline suggests. The single-year gap is statistically distinguishable from zero in seven of the ten years (2015–2020, plus 2023). In three years — 2021, 2022 and 2024 — the apparent gap is consistent with no gap at all.

Two things stand out. The non-White line moves more sharply than the White line: from 2017 to 2022 it roughly doubled (9.2% → 23.6%) while the White rate went up by half (16.7% → 25.6%). Expansion benefited everyone, but it benefited non-White applicants more in proportional terms. And in the pre-2020 era the bands are cleanly separated; from 2021 onwards they sit on top of each other in every year except 2023, when the lower edge of the White band just barely clears the upper edge of the non-White band. Translated into odds-ratio terms, every year from 2015 to 2020 has a White-advantage odds ratio with its entire confidence interval above parity. Three of the four most recent years have CIs that include parity; the gap is not distinguishable from zero. The exception is 2023, where the OR's lower bound is barely above 1.0.

The year-pair tests reinforce this. The long-run narrowing is real — the 2015 gap is significantly different from the 2024 gap, and the 2017-peak vs 2024 gap is strongly so. The 2022→2023 "widening" and the 2023→2024 "narrowing" are not statistically distinguishable from each other or from zero. The 2024 gap is statistically identical to 2022's. Treating the 2022–2024 sequence as a "reopening followed by partial closure" is reading signal into noise.

One last point worth making about the recent tightening. A standard decomposition of the 2022→2024 drop in overall acceptance rate (from 25.1% to 20.6%) shows that essentially all of it — to two decimal places — comes from rate change within ethnic groups, not from composition shift. The diversification of the applicant pool made almost no difference to the headline rate; the tightening came from how the gate was set, not from who was queuing at it. Whatever happened in 2023 and 2024 to bring rates down affected every group at roughly the same proportional rate.

In three of the four most recent years, the White–Non-White gap is consistent with zero. The 2023 outlier sits at the boundary of statistical significance. The "wobble" we earlier described as real movement is, on the formal test, statistically indistinguishable from noise.

03 / CompositionThe applicant pool is broadening — but not evenly

Behind the success-rate story sits a slower, steadier shift in who applies. The White share of applicants has fallen from 81 per cent in 2016 to 71 per cent in 2024 — a 10-point drop. Asian-heritage applicants have grown the most in relative terms, rising from 6 to 11 per cent. Mixed-heritage applicants are up from 4 to 6 per cent.

Black applicants are the laggard in this broader picture. Their share has crept from 4 to 6 per cent — real progress, but proportionally less than other groups, and stalled in the most recent years.

The composition shift is real — but it is unevenly distributed across non-White groups. The "broadening" of clinical psychology is driven primarily by Asian-heritage applicants and Mixed-heritage applicants. The Black applicant share is essentially flat over the last four years.

And one further note. The White share of acceptances falls more slowly than the White share of applicants. In 2024, White applicants made up 71 per cent of the applicant pool but 73 per cent of acceptances. This is the residue of differential success rates discussed above. When the system narrows, structural advantage shows up again.

04 / SexualityOut and openly so: the heterosexual share has dropped 15 points

The shift in sexual orientation among applicants is one of the cleanest trends in the data. In 2016 and 2017, nine in ten applicants identified as heterosexual. By 2024, fewer than three in four did.

The growth has come almost entirely from bisexual applicants. Their share more than quadrupled — from 3 per cent in 2016 to nearly 13 per cent in 2024. The shares of gay men and lesbians have held broadly steady at around 2 per cent each. There is now a meaningfully larger "other" group too.

How much of this reflects a real change in the applicant pool versus a change in willingness to disclose is impossible to tell from this dataset alone. Both forces are almost certainly in play. What is clear is that in 2024, more than a quarter of all clinical psychology applicants identify as something other than heterosexual.

05 / AgeThe largest gap in the data is not the one we have been discussing

The single biggest source of unequal outcomes in clinical psychology selection is age. An applicant in their late twenties has roughly 3.6 times the odds of acceptance of an applicant in their late forties. The penalty is monotonic above 30 and grows with age. By the standardised effect-size measures we use to rank dimensions, age dwarfs every other gap in the data by a factor of two or three.

Four gaps moved meaningfully during the expansion. Four others stayed put. Among the movers, the age penalty grew the most and the ethnicity gap shrank the most. Among the four that stayed put, class, gender, and disability are the most stubborn — and the least discussed.

The arrows above sort by how much each gap changed between the pre-expansion and expansion eras. The top four moved substantially. The bottom four did not.

The age penalty grew the most. Applicants under 40 were 1.8 times as likely to be accepted as applicants 40 and over in 2015–19; by 2020–24 they were 2.2 times as likely. This is counterintuitive — you might expect a system handing out more places to be more generous to all candidates. The most likely explanation is composition. The expansion drew in many more applicants in their twenties, so the "average competitive candidate" got younger, and the 40+ applicant looks comparatively further from that average. Absolute success rates for 40+ applicants did improve in the expansion era (9.1% to 10.4%) — they just didn't keep pace with the under-40 cohort's much bigger improvement (16.3% to 22.9%). The granular detail is also worth seeing: applicants in their 30s succeed at lower rates than those under 30 in both eras, but the 30s cohort benefited from the expansion at roughly the same proportional rate as the under-30s did. The penalty really kicks in at 40, not 30.

The Brexit cliff was actually larger before Brexit took effect. UK applicants were 2.6 times as likely to be accepted as EU/EEA applicants in 2015–19, falling to 2.0 times in 2020–24 — the opposite of what most "Brexit caused this" narratives would predict. The most likely explanation is that the EU applicant pool was already structurally different from the UK pool (older on average, more likely to be applying as international students, less likely to have UK clinical experience), and Brexit-era policy changes mostly accelerated a self-selection process that was already underway. By 2020–24 the remaining EU applicants were closer in profile to UK ones, so the underlying gap looked smaller.

The ethnicity gap is the only large gap that visibly contracts. White applicants were 1.5 times as likely to be accepted as non-White applicants in 2015–19. By 2020–24 they were 1.1 times — barely above parity. The shrinking arrow on row three is the single most consequential thing in this chart, and the only contrast that meaningfully moved toward parity.

The dependants penalty grew. This is probably the age effect in disguise: applicants with dependants tend to be older, and the age penalty steepened over the same period. Without joint distributions of age and parental status, the two can't be separated cleanly.

The structural four. Below the dividing line, four gaps barely moved. Socio-economic background (most vs least advantaged postcodes) stays around 1.5 to 1.6 times in both eras. LGB+ applicants succeed at a modest premium over heterosexual applicants in both periods, growing only marginally. Female vs male sits at a small structural female advantage. Disabled vs non-disabled is barely above parity in both, although as Section 06b shows, this aggregate hides genuine sub-type differences.

The headline: most of the unequal outcomes in clinical psychology selection are structural features of the system, not artifacts of any one era. The ethnicity gap is the major exception — substantially dismantled during the expansion. Whether that survives whatever comes next will be the most important thing to watch in the next few years of Clearing House data.

06 / DisabilityDisability disclosure has nearly doubled — driven by mental health

The share of applicants disclosing a disability has risen from 9 per cent in 2016 to nearly 17 per cent in 2024. That is the steepest rise of any single demographic indicator in the data, save the bisexual share.

The growth is not evenly spread across categories. Dyslexia disclosures have grown modestly. Disclosures of physical disabilities (mobility, sensory) have not moved much in absolute terms. The story is concentrated in two categories: mental health difficulties, which have nearly quadrupled, and multiple disabilities, which has tripled.

This is not, in itself, evidence that more applicants have disabilities. It is consistent with rising willingness to disclose, with mental health categories being normalised, and with changing diagnostic patterns in the wider population — all of which we know to be happening. But the implication for course providers is direct: a clinical psychology cohort in 2024 looks meaningfully different from one in 2016, and accommodations and support arrangements have to keep pace.

Three disability sub-types succeed at noticeably higher rates than non-disabled applicants. Applicants with two or more disabilities have a 25.1 per cent success rate pooled across the years the category has been published. Applicants with mental-health difficulties have a 24.3 per cent rate — though this is mostly a post-2020 phenomenon: the trajectory was flat through 2019 and has been consistently above the non-disabled rate since. Applicants with an "unseen" disability such as diabetes, epilepsy or asthma have a 23.5 per cent rate. All three sit several points above the non-disabled reference of around 21 per cent.

Dyslexia, the largest single sub-type, sits essentially at the non-disabled rate. This is what was making the aggregate look flat — dyslexia accounts for so much of the disabled total that its rate-neutrality dominates the average. Why this pattern? A handful of hypotheses are consistent with the data, though none is testable from the Clearing House figures alone. Disability sub-types with higher acceptance rates may attract applicants for whom the condition is a meaningful part of their professional identity, who self-select on attributes that correlate with selection success. Clinical psychology is one of the few professions in which lived experience of mental ill-health can be presented as relevant professional preparation; the post-2020 emergence of the mental-health pattern is consistent with selection panels increasingly recognising this. Applicants with chronic illness or multiple disabilities may also skew younger, and younger applicants succeed at higher rates — so part of the pattern may be the age effect in disguise. The data here can identify the pattern; it cannot explain it.

The headline should not be "disabled applicants do better than non-disabled applicants." It should be that the binary split is the wrong unit of analysis. Different sub-types behave differently, and reporting "disability has no effect" averages out information that matters.

07 / StasisSome things have not moved at all

For all the shifts above, two things are conspicuously immobile in this dataset.

The first is gender. In 2016, 82 per cent of applicants were women. In 2024, 82 per cent of applicants were women. The female share of acceptances is, if anything, slightly higher than the female share of applicants in most years. This is a profession that recruits, accepts and trains overwhelmingly from one half of the population, and has done so consistently for at least nine years.

The second is the socio-economic backdrop. The Clearing House used to publish breakdowns by POLAR quintile — a measure of how likely young people from a postcode are to enter higher education. They stopped publishing this in the 2023 entry data, switching to a different "contextual admissions" methodology. While the series ran, the picture was stubborn. Quintile 5 (the most advantaged) consistently supplied roughly a quarter of all applicants. Quintile 1 (the least advantaged) supplied around 8 to 9 per cent. The gap narrowed modestly over six years; it did not close.

The flat-line on gender stands in pointed contrast to the convergence-and-divergence story on ethnicity. Different demographic dimensions of the same system are moving at different speeds, in different directions, and for different reasons. Anyone treating "clinical psychology is becoming more diverse" as a single trend will miss most of what's actually happening.

08 / RepresentationCompared to whom? Set against the UK population, the applicant pool tells a different story.

A year-on-year time series tells you whether a profession is changing. It does not tell you whether it has arrived. For that, you need a comparator. So we set the 2024 Clearing House data against the 2021 UK Census of England and Wales — the closest population baseline available — and the result is genuinely surprising.

On ethnicity, the headline finding from earlier in this analysis is that the White share of applicants has fallen 10 points since 2016. The natural follow-up question is: where does that leave the applicant pool relative to the country it draws from? The answer, in 2024, is that the clinical psychology applicant pool is now more ethnically diverse than the UK working-age population. Asian, Black, Mixed-heritage and "Other" ethnic groups are all overrepresented at the applicant stage relative to their share of 16- to 64-year-olds in England and Wales. The White share of applicants (71.3 per cent) sits below the White share of the working-age population (80.7 per cent).

This is a striking finding for a profession whose historic image has been overwhelmingly white. And it complicates the standard "diversity gap" narrative. The gap at the applicant stage is not that minoritised candidates are missing. They are present in numbers that exceed their population share. The gap is downstream of that — in success rates, in acceptances, and in the profession's senior ranks, which the Clearing House data does not capture.

Read left to right and the story is sequential. From the population to the applicant pool, the White share drops by 7.8 percentage points; from applicants to acceptances, it climbs back by 1.6 points. The minority groups move the other way: more numerous among applicants than in the population, but slightly less numerous among acceptances than among applicants. That second movement, the regression toward whiteness at the acceptance stage, is what the differential success-rate chart from Section 02 was telling us — visible here in the composition itself.

None of this is to say the profession has "solved" representation. The cohort of trainees beginning in 2024 is still less White than the working-age population, but the field of qualified clinical psychologists — which reflects 30 years of cumulative intake — looks very different. The British Psychological Society reported in 2023 that around 92 per cent of its members identified as White. The composition of trainees is the future of the workforce; it is not the present of it.

In 2024, ethnic minority applicants exceed their share of the UK working-age population. The gap is no longer at the application stage. It is at the stages that follow.

09 / Representation, IIThe fuller picture: where the applicant pool diverges most from the country

Two findings stand out. The applicant pool is dramatically more LGB+ than the UK adult population — by a factor of nearly six. And it is strikingly more female than the population — by a factor of 1.6. Almost nothing else is more than 50 per cent off parity.

The LGB+ ratio dwarfs everything else on the chart. Even compared to 16- to 24-year-olds — the demographic with the highest LGB+ share in the UK at 8 per cent — clinical psychology applicants in 2024 are 2.4 times more LGB+. There is no other large profession the author can identify whose entry-level pool is this distant from the general population on this dimension.

The interpretation is contested. One hypothesis worth investigating is that clinical psychology training selects on attributes — interest in mental health, comfort with personal disclosure, willingness to interrogate identity — that correlate with sexual-minority status in survey data. Applicants are also younger and more disabled than the general adult population, both of which independently correlate with higher LGB+ disclosure rates. Whether the magnitude of the over-representation is best explained by self-selection of LGB+ candidates into the field, by higher disclosure rates within an already-selected pool, or by something else, the data here cannot say. What it can say is that the field is unambiguously not representative of the UK adult population on this dimension.

The gender skew, by contrast, is the smaller surprise. Female applicants outnumber male applicants by five to one in this dataset. But that ratio is essentially identical to the female-to-male ratio of UK psychology undergraduates, where women have made up around 80 per cent of acceptances onto psychology degrees for several years. The gender imbalance in clinical psychology training does not originate at the doctorate application stage. It is inherited, unchanged, from the discipline's undergraduate cohort. Anyone hoping for change at the training-place stage is looking in the wrong place.

Two omissions are worth flagging. First, the Clearing House does not currently report socio-economic background against a population baseline; the older POLAR-quintile breakdowns hinted that the most-advantaged quintile supplied roughly a quarter of applicants, which is broadly proportionate, but the data is no longer published in a form that allows direct comparison. Second, this analysis compares England-and-Wales census figures to UK-wide applicants. The mismatch is small (Scottish and Northern Irish applicants are a small share) but it is a caveat.

10 / CodaWhat the profession talks about, and what the data says it should

The Clearing House data is a useful corrective to easy narratives. "Clinical psychology is becoming more diverse" is true on some axes, false on others, and most consequential where it sits underneath a much larger gap the profession has barely started discussing. With that in mind, three signals to follow when the 2025 and 2026 entry data are published.

First, the age penalty. The largest single source of unequal outcomes in this dataset is age. An applicant in their late forties has about a quarter of the odds of acceptance of an applicant in their late twenties, and the penalty grew during the pandemic-era expansion rather than shrinking. Whether this reflects direct selection-against-age or indirect selection on age-correlated attributes the data cannot say, but the size of the effect — two to three times larger than any other gap in the data — argues that any serious widening-participation work needs to look at it specifically, and the profession's existing diversity conversation does not yet do that.

Second, the ethnicity gap. The closure that began in 2021 has held through 2024 on the formal test, with three of the four most recent years showing CIs that include parity. If 2025 looks like the post-2020 pattern, the closure can plausibly be called durable. If it looks more like 2017, we have a regime shift back. The OR and its CI are the signal to watch — the bare percentage gap can move a point or two year-on-year without meaning anything.

Third, what disclosure is measuring. The sharp rises in disability and non-heterosexual identification almost certainly mix two different things: changes in who is applying, and changes in what applicants are willing to say. The two are not the same. The downstream version of this question — what the qualified workforce looks like and how it compares to the applicant pool — is the one that ultimately matters, and the data that would answer it does not exist in a comparable form. It should.

The headline numbers from ten years of Clearing House data describe a profession that is clearly broader than it was. The fine grain describes a profession whose broadening is uneven, structurally tethered to how many training places the NHS chooses to commission, and whose biggest equity question has very little to do with the demographics it currently talks about. The next few years of data will tell us which of those characterisations is the more durable.

Methodology & notes

Primary source. All applicant and acceptance figures come from the Clearing House for Postgraduate Courses in Clinical Psychology, "Equal Opportunities Data" reports for entry years 2015 through 2024, available at clearing-house.org.uk. The Clearing House handles applications to all UK NHS-funded clinical psychology doctorate courses; "application" here means an applicant who submitted at least one course choice in a given entry year.

What this dataset covers — and does not. The Clearing House data covers one stage of a multi-stage selection process. Applicants choose which courses to apply to, courses then shortlist, interview, and make offers, and applicants either accept or decline. The "success rate" used throughout this analysis is the application-to-acceptance funnel: it does not separately measure shortlisting rates, interview-stage rates, offer-to-acceptance conversion, or course-level patterns. Where this analysis says "the system selects" or "the gap exists at selection", that is shorthand for "the gap is visible in the application-to-acceptance funnel" — the specific stage at which a gap originates is not identifiable from these data alone.

Comparator sources. Population benchmarks in Sections 08 and 09 use the ONS 2021 Census of England and Wales (ethnicity, sexual orientation, disability, gender), the ONS Annual Population Survey for 16–24-year-old LGB+ rates, and HESA undergraduate-level statistics for the psychology-graduate gender comparator. The 2021/22 Scottish Census used slightly different methodology and is excluded; comparisons use England-and-Wales as the primary benchmark.

Calculations. "Success rate" is acceptances divided by applicants for the relevant group. "LGB+" is the sum of bisexual, gay, lesbian and "other sexual orientation" categories. The representation-ratio chart normalises applicant percentages by excluding "prefer not to say" where comparable to the census categorisation.

Statistical methods. Confidence intervals on success rates use the Wilson score method at 95 per cent. Odds ratios use a 0.5 continuity correction with Wald log-odds CIs. Year-pair comparisons test the difference in log odds ratios via a z-statistic. The Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition splits change in acceptance rate into rate, composition and interaction components additively. The forest plot in Section 05 uses Benjamini–Hochberg FDR adjustment across its family of contrasts. The disability-sub-type chart in Section 06b is presented descriptively, without inferential statistics, because several cell sizes are small enough that adjusted CIs and significance markers would be misleading.

Interpretation of CIs. Clearing House data is closer to a census of consenting applicants than a random sample, so classical sampling-theory CIs do not strictly apply. CIs here should be read as a noise envelope — the range of rates one would plausibly observe if the admissions process were rerun under similar conditions — not as classical sampling intervals from a random sample.

What we cannot do with this data. The Clearing House publishes only marginal counts by characteristic. We do not have the joint distribution of age, ethnicity, sex and other characteristics for any given applicant, so multivariate analysis ("controlling for X") is impossible. We cannot track individuals across years, and we do not know the consent rate, so we cannot directly assess non-response selection bias. POLAR quintile data was discontinued after the 2022 entry data, so post-2022 socio-economic analysis is not possible in a comparable form. We cannot observe what happens after acceptance — completion, dropout, qualification, or workforce entry are all out of scope.

Caveats. Rises in disclosure rates (especially for disability and sexuality) cannot be cleanly separated from rises in underlying prevalence within the applicant pool. Categorisation changes in religion and ethnicity across Clearing House years exist but are minor for the broad analyses used here. The representation-ratio comparison treats the 2021 Census as a stable baseline for 2024.
